TL
Executive TL;DR
The whole activity, explained like you have thirty seconds
  • What is this? Teams scan the live Auralab network to figure out what devices are actually connected to it.
  • Why bother? Before you can secure or attack anything, you have to know what's there. Network discovery is step one of every real engagement.
  • Is it dangerous? No. Every device on Auralab belongs to this class. Nobody is scanning anything outside this room.
  • What do they do all day? Run a ping sweep, then act like a detective: sort real targets from the noise of three other teams' Kali boxes.
  • What tools do they need? A terminal and Nmap. Nothing else.
  • What do they walk away with? A defensible answer to "how many devices are on this network," and evidence to back it up.

Authorization & Team Structure
Read this before you open a terminal
⚠
Scan Auralab only

The Auralab network exists specifically for this class to practice on, legally and safely. You are authorized to scan Auralab and only Auralab. Never point Nmap, or any scanning tool, at a network you do not own or have explicit written permission to test.

Four teams, Ghost, Shadow, Fantom, and Cipher, are all connecting Kali machines to Auralab at the same time as you. A printer, a PC, and a Raspberry Pi are also on the network. Exactly how many devices total is something you will work out, not something you will be told.

ℹ
The Feynman rule

No team reports a final host count until at least one member can explain, in their own words, the specific evidence behind every host they classified. A guess is not a finding. Evidence is.

01
What Is a Ping Sweep?
The first move in any network discovery job

A ping sweep asks every address on a network range one simple question: are you there? It doesn't check ports or services, it just finds out who answers. The result is your starting roster, a list of IP and MAC addresses you still have to make sense of.

$ ip a
$ nmap -sn 192.168.1.0/24
  • ip a shows your own IP address and interface. An address like 192.168.1.42 tells you the network range is 192.168.1.0/24.
  • nmap -sn runs a ping-only sweep across that whole range, no port scan.
  • A plain nmap <target-ip>, with no flags, runs Nmap's default port scan against one specific host.
02
Key Vocabulary
Words you need before you run a single scan
Ping sweep
A scan that checks which addresses on a range respond at all, with no port or service detail.
TTL (Time to Live)
A counter in every packet that decreases by one per network hop. Its starting value differs by OS family and is visible in scan output.
MAC address / OUI
A hardware address tied to the network card. Its first three bytes (the OUI) identify the manufacturer.
Open port
A network service actively listening for connections on a host. The combination of open ports is often a strong fingerprint.
Host count confidence
How sure you can be that a single scan reflects every device really on the network, versus devices that simply didn't answer that time.
03
Fingerprint Clues: What Each One Tells You
No single clue is proof by itself, combine them
ClueWhat it tells you
TTLLinux-family systems default to 64. Windows defaults to 128. Network gear and some embedded devices default to 255.
MAC vendor prefixThe OUI, the first three bytes of a MAC address, identifies the manufacturer of the network card.
HostnameIf a device broadcasts a name, it can be a direct giveaway, but not every device does.
Open portsPort 22 alone suggests a lean Linux box. Port 631 suggests a printer. Ports 135, 139, and 445 together suggest Windows.
Host countA single sweep only shows who answered at that moment. A missing device may be filtering ping, not absent.
S1
Step 1: Connect & Find Yourself
Get on the network before you scan it
▾
⚠
Use the Auralab password given to you in class

Connect your Kali machine to the Auralab Wi-Fi network before doing anything else.

Steps
  • Open a terminal.
  • Run ip a to find your own IP address and interface.
  • Identify your network range. An address like 192.168.1.42 means the network is 192.168.1.0/24.
ℹ
Write your own IP down now

You will need to cross yourself off the list in Step 3.

S2
Step 2: Run the Ping Sweep
Build your starting roster
▾

A ping sweep asks every address on the network one question: are you there? It doesn't check ports or services, just who answers.

$ nmap -sn 192.168.1.0/24

This returns a list of IP and MAC addresses. That list is your starting roster.

ℹ
Record everything before moving on

Write every IP and MAC address on your Network Forensics Worksheet. You'll need the full list for Step 3.

S3
Step 3: Sort the Noise
Tell other teams' Kali boxes apart from real unknowns
▾

Four teams are running Kali on similar hardware at the same time as you. Most of what showed up in your sweep is probably another team's machine, not a hidden device. Cross off your own IP from Step 1, then use the Fingerprint Clues table to make a call on everything that's left: another team's Kali box, or something else.

ℹ
A guess without a reason isn't a finding

For each host, write down the specific clue that led you there.

S4
Step 4: Find Open Ports
Fingerprint what's still unaccounted for
▾

Set aside the hosts you identified as other teams' machines. For each host still unaccounted for, run a default scan and see what's listening.

$ nmap <target-ip>

The pattern of open ports is a fingerprint, see the Fingerprint Clues table above for what common ports suggest.

S5
Step 5: Re-Sweep for Consistency
Check whether your snapshot holds up
▾

A single sweep is a snapshot, not proof. Wait a few minutes, then run the same ping sweep again.

$ nmap -sn 192.168.1.0/24
ℹ
Did the same hosts answer both times?

A host that drops out between sweeps is still worth a note, it may just be filtering ping, not gone.

S6
Step 6: Wrap Up
Turn evidence into a defensible answer
▾

Talk with your team before you write anything down.

  • Before anything is confirmed: how many devices do you believe are on this network, and how confident are you?
  • What would make you more or less confident in that number?
  • What's the difference between a host that didn't answer and a host that isn't there?
ℹ
Where this goes

Write your team's answers on the Network Forensics Worksheet.
