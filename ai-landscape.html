Self-Directed Activity · Unit 1 of 7 · 100 Points

The AI Landscape

Survey the AI tool ecosystem — what tools exist, what they do, where they fail, and how they might work together. The mental model you build here is the foundation for everything that follows.

Phase 1 — Literacy 100 Points 5 Activities 2 Weeks Individual No Accounts Until Activity 2
Overview
Unit Brief

Right now, there are hundreds of AI tools in use — tools for writing, searching, generating images, coding, scheduling, and summarizing. Most people use one or two without really knowing what they are or why they work the way they do. That gap is the problem this unit is here to fix.

Over two weeks you will explore the AI ecosystem systematically — comparing tools side by side, testing their limits, and starting to think about how tools can connect into larger workflows. By the end, you will have built your own AI Ecosystem Map: a visual document of everything you found, analyzed, and designed.

  • Essential Questions
  • What is an AI tool, and how is it different from other software I use every day?
  • Why do so many AI tools exist — and how do I choose the right one for a task?
  • What does it mean for AI tools to "work together," and why would that be useful?
  • What You Will Turn In
  • AI Ecosystem Map — a visual map of 8+ tools organized by category with annotations
  • Strengths & limitations notes for at least 4 tools from your research
  • One workflow sketch connecting 2+ tools with labeled inputs and outputs
  • Written reflection — 1 page using unit vocabulary, submitted with your map
  • Tool log for up to 5 bonus points — every tool you visited, with a one-sentence note on what it does
Learning Objectives
  • Name the major categories of AI tools and give a representative example of each
  • Explain how a large language model differs from a search engine in your own words
  • Compare outputs from ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini using structured prompts and identify meaningful differences
  • Define AI hallucination and explain why it matters for how you use these tools
  • Match tools to tasks based on their specific strengths and limitations
  • Sketch a simple multi-tool AI workflow for a task from your own academic life
Activities
01 Survey — Mapping the AI Ecosystem 20 pts

Before you can use AI tools well, you need to know what's out there. In this activity you will research the major categories of AI tools and build a working picture of the landscape.

  • 1.1What are the four main categories of AI tools? For each category, name one example and write one sentence describing what it does. (Categories to consider: conversational AI, search AI, image generation, productivity AI.)
  • 1.2What is a large language model (LLM)? Explain how it works and how it is different from a traditional search engine. Write in your own words — no copy/paste.
  • 1.3Choose two tools from different categories and compare them directly. What is each one best at? What is each one not well suited for? Give at least two specifics per tool.
  • 1.4Name the company behind ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. Why might different ownership affect how each tool behaves, what data it uses, or who it is designed to serve?
Research Tip

Use Perplexity.ai or a web search to research this activity — not the tools themselves. If you ask ChatGPT about ChatGPT, you will get a one-sided answer. Use an outside source so your information is more objective.

02 Compare — Same Prompt, Three Tools 20 pts

The best way to understand AI tools is to use them side by side. You will run the same prompts through ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini and analyze the differences. You will need a free account for each tool before starting this activity.

  • 2.1Run this prompt in ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini: "Explain the water cycle in two paragraphs for a 9th grader." Record or screenshot all three responses. Identify at least three specific differences in how the tools responded — in length, tone, accuracy, or structure.
  • 2.2Now run a prompt of your own on a topic you are currently studying in another class. Which tool gave you the most useful response for academic work? Explain why using specific details from the responses.
  • 2.3Which tool's writing style did you personally prefer, and why? Is "preferred style" always the same as "most accurate"? Can you have a preference for a tool that is actually less reliable?
  • 2.4Based on this experiment, what does it tell you about why choosing the right tool for the right task matters? Give one specific example of a task you would send to one tool but not the others.
Setup Note

Free accounts: ChatGPT at chat.openai.com · Claude at claude.ai · Gemini at gemini.google.com. Copy each response into a shared document so you can compare them side by side. Do not edit or summarize the responses before comparing.

03 Research — Search AI vs. Chat AI 20 pts

Not all AI tools approach information the same way. Some generate responses from memory alone. Others actively search the web and cite their sources in real time. This difference matters enormously the moment you start using AI for research.

  • 3.1Use Perplexity.ai to research this question: "What are the most commonly used AI tools in high school classrooms right now?" Describe what you found and list at least two sources that Perplexity cited. Do the sources seem credible?
  • 3.2Ask the same question in ChatGPT. How does the response differ from Perplexity? Does ChatGPT provide sources? How does the absence of citations affect how much you trust the answer?
  • 3.3What is a "knowledge cutoff"? Why does it matter for research tasks? Find and record the knowledge cutoff date for at least two AI tools you have used this week.
  • 3.4For which types of school tasks would you choose Perplexity over ChatGPT? For which would you do the opposite? Give a specific example of each and explain your reasoning.
On AI and Research Responsibility

Using an AI for research does not mean the AI is researching for you. It means you are evaluating AI output the way you would evaluate any other source. Citation, verification, and critical judgment are still your responsibility — no tool removes that obligation.

04 Analyze — Strengths, Limits, and Failure Modes 20 pts

Every AI tool has a ceiling. Understanding where tools fail is just as important — maybe more important — than knowing what they can do. In this activity you will explore hallucination, bias, and the art of matching tools to tasks.

  • 4.1What is AI "hallucination"? Define it clearly in your own words using the Feynman Technique — explain it so well that someone who has never heard of AI would understand it. Then give one concrete example of what hallucination looks like in practice.
  • 4.2Find or produce an actual example of an AI hallucination. What prompt caused it? What did the AI claim? What is actually true? Include a screenshot or quoted text as evidence.
  • 4.3For each task below, identify which AI tool from this unit you would use and explain why in one sentence: (a) writing a first draft of a history essay, (b) finding recent news about a current event, (c) brainstorming ten ideas for a school project, (d) checking whether a historical fact is accurate, (e) summarizing a long article you have already found.
  • 4.4Name one task you should never fully trust any AI to do without human verification. Justify your answer with a specific example of what could go wrong if someone trusted the output without checking.
Framing This Correctly

Understanding limits is not a reason to distrust AI — it is a reason to use it smarter. The goal of this activity is not to conclude that AI is unreliable. The goal is to know precisely where it is strong, where it is weak, and how your own judgment fills the gap.

05 Design — Your First Workflow Sketch 20 pts

Individual AI tools are useful. AI tools that work together — passing outputs from one step to the next — can handle tasks no single tool could manage alone. This is where the real opportunity lives. In this activity you will design your first workflow.

  • 5.1What does "AI orchestration" mean? Write a one-paragraph definition in your own words. Include an example of a task that would benefit from two or more AI tools working in sequence rather than just one tool doing everything.
  • 5.2An AI pipeline has three layers: AI tools (the brains), productivity platforms (the destinations — Google Docs, Notion, Canva, etc.), and connectors (the bridges — tools like Zapier or Make that link them). Name one real example of each layer from your own experience or research.
  • 5.3Think of a task you actually do in school — writing a research paper, preparing for a test, organizing notes, completing a project. Sketch a workflow that uses at least two AI tools to help you do it. Label the input (what you start with), each tool in the sequence, and the output (what you end up with). Your sketch can be digital or on paper.
  • 5.4What is one problem that could arise when AI tools pass information to each other automatically, without you checking in between? What would you need to verify to make sure the final output was still accurate and trustworthy?
Keep It Simple

Your workflow does not need to be complicated. A three-step pipeline — search a topic with Perplexity, summarize the results with Claude, paste the summary into Google Docs — is a complete and valid workflow. The goal here is to think about connections, not to build anything yet. Building comes in Unit 4.

Final Deliverable — AI Ecosystem Map

Your work across all five activities comes together in one document: your AI Ecosystem Map. This is a visual + written artifact that shows what you now understand about the AI landscape.

Visual Map

8+ AI tools, organized by category, with short descriptions. Digital (Canva, Google Slides) or hand-drawn and scanned — your choice.

Strengths & Limits

Annotations for at least 4 tools showing at least one strength and one real limitation backed by something you observed or researched.

Workflow Sketch

One diagram connecting 2+ tools with labeled inputs and outputs. Drawn from Activity 5 — refined based on peer feedback.

Written Reflection

One page using unit vocabulary. What surprised you? What changed in how you think about AI? What do you still want to understand?

  • Written reflection (1 page minimum) included in the same submission or as a separate document
  • Responses to all five activity question sets — these can be in a single shared Google Doc or submitted in your LMS
  • Workflow sketch from Activity 5 — included in your map or as a separate labeled diagram
  • Tool log for up to 5 bonus points — a running list of every AI tool you encountered, with one sentence on what it does and whether you used it
Starter Resources

These are starting points — you are encouraged to find others. Use them to verify what tools tell you about themselves.

  • Perplexity.ai — Use for any research question in this unit; it cites sources so you can verify what it tells you
  • MIT Technology Review — Search "AI tools 2025" for current, credible coverage of what is available and what it does
  • Google AI Blog (blog.google) — Explains Gemini and Google's AI approach from the source
  • Anthropic.com/news — Background on Claude, how it was built, and the principles behind it
  • OpenAI.com/research — Overview of ChatGPT and GPT model capabilities, directly from the source
  • Zapier Blog — Search "AI workflow automation" for clear explainers on how tools connect — useful for Activity 5
  • The Verge / Wired — Search "AI hallucination examples" for documented, real-world cases useful for Activity 4.2
Source Quality

Avoid using any single AI tool as your only source for information about that tool. Company blogs are useful but self-promotional. Look for third-party reviews, news coverage, and academic or government sources when you want a less biased picture.
